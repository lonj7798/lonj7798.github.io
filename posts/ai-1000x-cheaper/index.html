The token factory — what a 1000x cost cut actually rests on

How giving up per-token latency unlocks cheaper chips, sites and power in Neil Movva's argument, which of his numbers survive a source check, and what would settle his forecast.

2026-08-30 ·18 min read ·20 min listen ·#deep-dive#ai-infra

In brief

In an Invest Like The Best interview published 2026-08-25, Neil Movva, co-founder of Sail Research and a former NVIDIA kernel engineer, argues that AI inference is about to get drastically cheaper because its dominant workload will change, from chatbots a person waits on to background agents that run for hours. Once nobody is waiting, a provider can batch for throughput instead of latency, use chips without NVIDIA's NVLink interconnect, tolerate data centers with 95 percent uptime, and site them on solar and wind. This article follows that chain layer by layer, checks the talk's numbers against primary sources, and separates what is delivered (about one order of magnitude on public price lists) from what is a roadmap (the three to six orders of magnitude the speaker asks for). It matters now because the first checkpoint, a 50/50 split between background and real-time workloads, is dated to the end of 2026.

  • The whole thesis follows one decision: stop optimizing for the person at the keyboard.
  • The hardware physics checks out: SRAM density, HBM bandwidth, tensor-parallel scaling and rack power all match sources.
  • The KV cache, not FLOPs, is the binding constraint; it also caps the batch sizes the thesis needs.
  • Public prices show about one order of magnitude, not three to six; the rest is roadmap.
  • Every claim about Sail's fleet, uptime and power is self-reported by a founder two months after an $80 million raise.
  • Watch batch-tier token share, METR's 80 percent horizons, HBM prices and open-model precision.

A founder's supply-side thesis, two months after an $80 million raise

Neil Movva is co-founder and CEO of Sail Research, a San Francisco inference company that emerged from stealth on 2026-06-25 with $80 million of funding at a reported $450 million valuation [2]. He joined NVIDIA while still in college and worked on GPU kernels there around 2015 to 2017 13:09, 16:58, [2], then moved to on-device computer vision at Apple and was the first kernels hire at Together AI [3]. Sail sells an API for open-weight models such as GLM, DeepSeek and Kimi, plus persistent sandboxes for agents that run for hours or days [4]; Movva calls it "a token factory" 0:38.

Two framings need separating, because the 1000x is the channel's title: Movva's own figures are that a tenfold price cut creates a new product category 1:13 and that a trillion tokens per person per day needs "three to six orders of magnitude" of cost reduction 1:15:11, a requirement, not a result. He is the founder of the company the episode describes, on a venture-run show whose disclaimer notes that clients may hold positions in companies discussed [1]. Every number about Sail's fleet, uptime and costs below is self-reported; only the funding, the March 2026 launch and the public rate card are confirmed [2], [4].

The bet starts with demand: agents that nobody is waiting for

The argument opens with usage, not hardware. Baseten, Fireworks and Together built for low latency because one customer, Cursor, pulled them there, Movva says; that was right a year ago, but for six months persistence and long tasks have mattered more 3:30.

4:22 "One way to say it is like the best latency is no latency at all. When you wake up in the morning, the work's already been done overnight."

Neil Movva

He gives three reasons. Test-time compute, more thinking time for a better answer 4:52, went public with OpenAI's o1 in September 2024 [5] and, in his view, became something to bet on with Claude Opus 4.5, released 2025-11-24 [6]; today an hour-long run is reliable and days are not 5:22. Background demand has no human bottleneck: he cannot use ten times more tokens in Codex or Claude Code himself, but a background job can 5:57. And the workloads exist: deep research over ten thousand sources, a customer indexing the whole web 6:28, security teams running frontier models against all their code 7:35. Then the load-bearing number: half and half between background and real-time by the end of 2026, then 90 to 10 in favor of background 5:57, 6:28.

The outside record fits the timing and is more cautious. METR, a non-profit that measures how long a task an agent can finish, puts Opus 4.5 at a 50 percent-success task horizon of about five hours but an 80 percent-success horizon near half an hour; Opus 4.6 reaches about 14.5 hours at 50 percent on a nearly saturated suite [7], [8]. Anthropic's 2026-02-18 autonomy study reports a median agent turn near 45 seconds, with the 99.9th percentile nearly doubling from under 25 minutes to over 45 in three months [9]. No provider publishes a background-versus-real-time split. Our reading: the labs describe a fat tail growing fast; Movva describes the tail becoming the body, and only his version justifies the fleet below.

Giving up latency releases a chain of constraints, one layer at a time

The GPU, Movva says, is fundamentally a throughput machine, happiest with a deep queue of work 18:04.

18:37 "There's a fundamental trade-off on the GPU between being […] throughput oriented or latency optimized and everyone has chosen latency optimization because the shape of usage was chatbot oriented."

Neil Movva

The mechanism is batching: a GPU reaches peak utilization only when many requests share one pass through the weights, and each request in a large batch waits for the others, a bus against a private car 19:47. The latency-first escape is tensor parallelism, splitting one matrix multiply across up to eight GPUs over NVLink, NVIDIA's interconnect. Each finishes sooner but not eight times sooner: four to five times, Movva says, because of communication overhead and smaller per-GPU tiles 21:32; published measurements say 4.5 to 6 [10]. If nobody is waiting, that lost efficiency is pure waste, so he prefers expert or pipeline parallelism, which place whole experts or layers on different chips and overlap communication with compute 21:32, 22:03. That admits other silicon: a chip without NVLink-class interconnect can still beat NVIDIA on floating-point operations (FLOPs) per dollar 22:37.

Seen whole, the talk is one causal chain: tolerate latency, batch deeply, keep the expert layers compute-bound, drop NVLink, buy any chip, accept unreliable sites and intermittent power. Our reading: each link reintroduces a cost at the next. Chips cheap enough to leave idle need low capital cost per FLOP, which rules out the 72-GPU GB300 NVL72 racks Movva himself calls the best throughput option today 44:26. The largest unstated coupling is memory. Each request in a batch carries its own key-value (KV) cache, the stored representation of every token in its context, so batch size is bounded by high-bandwidth memory (HBM) capacity divided by per-request cache. The long-context agents the thesis serves have the largest caches, and the talk never reconciles the two.

SRAM versus HBM is a contest between static and dynamic state

Cerebras and Groq, Movva says, bet on a different memory hierarchy: fill the chip with static random-access memory (SRAM), the six-transistor cell built on the logic die, instead of the dynamic RAM stacked beside it as HBM 24:04. SRAM is fast because it sits next to the arithmetic units and scarce because each cell is large. An entire 800 mm² Blackwell-class die of SRAM would hold single-digit gigabytes, against 288 GB of HBM around a Blackwell package and perhaps 500 MB of SRAM on it 24:04, 25:50. Cerebras uses the whole wafer, about 50 GB at roughly 21 petabytes per second, against about 10 terabytes per second for HBM 26:31, 27:08.

Those figures hold: Cerebras states 44 GB on its WSE-3 [11] and about 21 PB/s [12]; Blackwell Ultra carries 288 GB of HBM3e at 8 TB/s per GPU [13]; B200's on-die L2 cache is 126 MB [14], and adding L1, tensor memory and register files brings all its on-die SRAM to roughly 240 MB (our arithmetic from [14]), so 500 MB is high by about two times.

What decides the market is what each memory holds. Weights are fixed at deploy time, so a trillion-parameter model such as Kimi can be spread across wafers and streamed fast 27:48, [15]. The KV cache is sized by users times context, is unknown until runtime, and "frequently gets to be larger than the weights" 28:24, 29:02. That is the thorn for SRAM-only designs, which he expects to end up as accelerators beside GPUs 30:42.

30:42 "You want offchip memory for capacity and onchip memory for speed. We want to hybridize these two things."

Neil Movva

He calls the placement of a memory-bound attention layer beside a compute-bound multilayer-perceptron (MLP) layer in every transformer block the architecture's "original sin": no single chip serves both well 31:16. His cut is by layer, attention on the GPU and MLP weights on an SRAM chip, which he says he believes NVIDIA and Groq are doing 31:53. The hybrid has moved from prediction to product: NVIDIA licensed Groq's SRAM-based processor technology for about $20 billion on 2025-12-24 [16], and Google's TPU 8i pairs 288 GB of HBM with 384 MB of SRAM [17]. The layer split is his own; the mainstream cut is by phase, with NVIDIA's Dynamo framework scheduling prefill and decode on different GPUs [18]. One claim is wrong: Anthropic was not first to a million-token context 30:11; Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro offered one in private preview from 2024-02-15 [19], Anthropic in August 2025 [20].

The scavenger strategy buys what frontier labs find illegible

Movva's rule for chips is "no bad chips", only "bad pricing"; he will make any chip work at the right price 45:44. AMD's parts are good and under-programmed, an edge for a kernel team, though Meta and OpenAI have now bought heavily 46:52, each in a 6 GW, multi-generation AMD Instinct deal whose first gigawatt of MI450 GPUs starts in the second half of 2026 [21], [22].

The data-center layer inverts the usual economics. A gigawatt site is now essentially impossible in the United States, he argues, 100 MW increasingly hard, 10 MW at the edge and 1 MW plentiful 53:51. With liquid cooling a megawatt is about eight refrigerator-sized racks 55:03, consistent with the 132 to 140 kW a GB300 NVL72 rack draws [23]. Sail's planned sites will drop diesel backup, redundant power and fiber, and service-level agreements, and some may run at 95 percent uptime 56:54. He will be the first buyer, for two reasons. A control plane can move work elsewhere as long as failures are uncorrelated, which makes him "linearly happy" across 95, 98 and 99 percent 57:22; and a failed hour-long turn costs the agent only an extra minute or two, or ten 57:56.

58:34 "our average throughput is going to be very competitive, but our P99, our 99th percentile latency, it's not going to be controlled. It cannot be. And in return, I'll give you unbeatable economics."

Neil Movva

Power completes the chain: he would take 80 percent uptime at the right price, which makes solar and wind usable, since he can model the weather and move the workload before a site goes dark 59:02, 59:35. The chips at such a site will not be NVIDIA racks, and if they are cheap enough he does not mind them idle 59:35, 1:00:06. Hence the name: scavenge chips, then scavenge power; never bid against Anthropic or OpenAI for concentrated supply; build the best steel factory from "mini mills", not one monolithic plant 1:00:06, 1:00:42.

Our reading: interruptibility is the new part, and its hidden cost is stated nowhere. When a GPU is pulled, the sandbox state moves but the KV cache does not, so the receiving GPU recomputes the whole context, compute-bound work paid twice; weather outages are site-wide, so every session at a site re-prefills at once. That needs warm spare capacity, the idle capital Movva says he does not mind 1:00:06, minutes before saying that idle GPUs in private pools pain him physically 1:04:53. Whether Sail's control plane makes this net positive is asserted, never described.

The hardware numbers check out; the narrative claims lean one way

Movva's stack-rank of waste points at bytes and idle time, not FLOPs. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models already activate few parameters per token, frontier models near 1 percent, he thinks 1:03:39. The KV cache stores many kilobytes per token, off by an order of magnitude or two, and DeepSeek publishes roughly an order of magnitude of compression a year 1:04:15.

The record supports the memory story: multi-head latent attention cut DeepSeek-V2's cache by 93.3 percent in May 2024 [24]; DeepSeek-V4-Pro, in April 2026, needs 10 percent of V3.2's cache at one million tokens [25], about 9.6 GiB per million-token sequence in 16-bit precision against 83.9 GiB for a V3.2-style model [26]. A Llama-3-70B-class design with grouped-query attention stores about 320 KB per token, over 300 GB for the same sequence (our arithmetic). The density claim is half right: DeepSeek-V4-Pro and Kimi K2 run at 3 percent active [25], [15], and no disclosed model is near 1 percent.

The internet, about 30 trillion tokens of high-quality text or 300 trillion on a wider definition, has already been seen many times over, he says 36:14. The 300 trillion is Epoch AI's estimate of the effective stock of public text, whose exhaustion Epoch projects for 2026 to 2032 [27]; 30 trillion is what one frontier pre-training run consumes [28].

The contrarian take on chips is right for one number format. Performance per watt in BF16, the 16-bit format used for training, has barely moved from Hopper to Blackwell to Rubin or across the recent process nodes of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), Movva says, so losing TSMC would be a supply shock rather than a catastrophe 1:16:51. Hopper to Blackwell was a real gain of about 60 percent in dense BF16 per watt, 989 TFLOPS at 700 W against 2,250 at 1,000 W [29], [30]. Blackwell to Rubin is flat because NVIDIA spent its transistors on the 4-bit and 8-bit formats FP4 and FP8 [31], which is why NVIDIA's partners claim about ten times the tokens per megawatt for Vera Rubin at NVFP4 [32]. Both statements are true, and precision, as Movva admits, is the labs' choice 1:18:04.

His reading that memory makers avoid capex for a cyclical market 1:19:40 describes late 2025: Micron has raised fiscal 2026 capex above $25 billion [33], and SK hynix approved 54 trillion won for two fabs on 2026-08-07, with the DRAM fab's cleanroom due in June 2029 [34]. The shortage he is betting on persists; the intent behind it has changed.

The verifiable saving is one order of magnitude; the rest is a roadmap

The price anchor is OpenAI's list: a trillion tokens costs at least $5 million on GPT-5.5 or 5.6 1:15:11, exactly $5 per million input tokens on GPT-5.5 [35]. GPT-5.6 Sol, also $5 at list as late as 2026-07-30 [36], now carries a promotional $4 input price that OpenAI's page says runs at least through 2026-11-21 [35]; secondary trackers date the cut to the days around the episode's release, and we could not fetch a primary announcement. Sail lists GLM-5.2 at $0.80 per million input and $3.00 output, with completion-window tiers that trade turnaround time for a discount; the site states those discounts against other providers, about 5 to 35 percent for the default window and 60 to 80 percent for the deepest [4], not against its own list. That is $800,000 per trillion at list and, comparing the two stated ranges, very roughly $200,000 to $500,000 on the deepest tier (our arithmetic; Sail publishes no flex-tier rate card). Movva's "tens of thousands of dollars" for some model sizes 1:15:48 implies about five cents per million (our arithmetic), a model with a few billion active parameters, and no published rate.

Decomposed into multipliers (our analysis), the demonstrated part is about one to one and a half orders of magnitude: six times from serving an open model instead of a flagship, and roughly 1.5 to 5 times from the deepest completion-window tier relative to Sail's default (our inference from the ranges Sail states against other providers). Incumbents have both: NVIDIA reported on 2026-02-12 that providers including Baseten, Fireworks and Together cut cost per token by up to ten times versus Hopper on Blackwell with NVFP4 and Dynamo, the ten-times case on GB200 NVL72 [37]. He expects much better kernel-writing models within six months and agrees that the software edge erodes over time 41:25. The remaining levers, KV-cache compression, FLOPs per dollar on other silicon, cheaper sites and power, and recovered idle capacity, are each a plausible two to ten times; five compound to three orders of magnitude, and six requires a much smaller model. Our reading: the trajectory belongs to the industry, and the only part of Sail's plan no incumbent can copy, scavenged silicon on interruptible power, is the part nobody outside Sail has seen.

For teams that build agents rather than serve them, the actionable content is the workload split. Tag every request with whether a person is waiting and route the rest to a throughput tier; the discount exists today, and the ratio is measurable. Build agents that survive losing their GPU mid-turn, with durable state outside the model and checkpoints at tool boundaries, which matches Anthropic's harness guidance [38]. Choose long-running models by KV bytes per token as well as price; at one million tokens of context a Llama-3-class and a DeepSeek-V4-class design differ by 300 GB against 10 GB of HBM per agent. And track cost per completed task, tokens per task times price times one plus the retry rate, the arithmetic behind the spending caps Movva sees on Claude Code 50:58.

For the industry, NVIDIA is untroubled: Movva is bullish on it in the short term 1:16:17, and it will not sell tokens itself because Jensen Huang keeps a diverse set of GPU cloud providers, the so-called NeoClouds, competing for its chips 1:20:15, 1:20:46. His own levers, cache compression and KV offload to flash 1:14:04, are demand-side attacks on the HBM shortage the memory makers profit from. Sail also rides on open weights staying three to six months behind 1:10:10, and the labs are acting on the distillation he calls inevitable 1:11:18: Fable 5 ships with a distillation classifier [39].

Five signals will settle the forecast, the first by 2026-12-31

The 50/50 checkpoint comes first, four months away, and its proxy is token share on batch or completion-window tiers. If by mid-2027 Baseten, Fireworks and Together have not launched asynchronous tiers carrying most of their volume, the shift is late; if they have, Sail's edge must show in its fleet rather than its scheduler.

The one-hour agent must become a one-day agent before 95 percent uptime is cheap, because migration plus re-prefill is negligible only when the reliability-weighted horizon dwarfs a ten-minute hiccup. If METR's 80 percent-success horizon passes about four hours by mid-2027, three doublings from Opus 4.5's half hour at roughly a six-month cadence [7], the argument holds; if it keeps lagging, tail latency still matters.

Our reading is that the HBM-scarce regime expires around 2029, when SK hynix's Yongin DRAM fab and Micron's new capacity land [34], [33]. Chips that sidestep HBM have roughly a 30-month window; after that, HBM-rich NVIDIA silicon gets cheaper too, and no bad chips, only bad pricing, cuts against the scavenger as much as for him.

Precision is a fork Movva admits he does not control 1:17:30. If open frontier models ship at native FP4 or FP8 quality by 2027, NVIDIA's per-watt gains are real and older silicon loses value; if serving stays at BF16 and FP8, his flat-per-watt claim holds.

Open-weight lag caps the abundance story. If no open model reaches Mythos-class agentic capability by the end of 2027, Sail's tokens are second-tier intelligence and the thesis narrows to the verifiable-task niches it already serves.

Movva's closing claim is that there will always be demand for intelligence, and that the on-ramps are the product community's problem, not his 1:15:48. The talk earns its hearing on the physics, which is sound, and asks for faith on the demand curve, which nobody has measured. The first date on which that faith can be checked is 2026-12-31.
