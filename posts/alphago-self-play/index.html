Search Is the Teacher — Lessons From a $10K AlphaGo Rebuild

Eric Jang's blackboard rebuild of AlphaGo explains why LLM reinforcement learning is information-starved — and what a per-move teacher would change.

2026-08-31 ·17 min read ·18 min listen ·#deep-dive#rl

In brief

Eric Jang — VP of AI at 1X Technologies until January 2026, and before that a research scientist at Google DeepMind Robotics — spent part of a sabbatical rebuilding AlphaGo with modern tools on roughly $10,000 of donated compute, then walked Dwarkesh Patel through the whole stack in a two-and-a-half-hour blackboard lecture. His central argument is that Monte Carlo Tree Search is a policy improvement operator, manufacturing a better training target for every single move, so AlphaGo Zero's training collapses into stable supervised learning on labels that search keeps improving. LLM reinforcement learning, by contrast, scores an entire trajectory with roughly one bit of outcome signal, which is why it is so sample-hungry. The talk also carries an unusual transparency beat: four days after airing, Jang published an errata correcting his own on-air variance math, and this article follows the corrected version.

  • MCTS hands the learner one improved target per move; outcome RL delivers roughly one bit per trajectory.
  • AlphaGo Zero's loop is supervised learning on labels search keeps improving — no explicit TD machinery.
  • The rebuild cost about $10K but bootstrapped from KataGo; the from-scratch run was still unvalidated.
  • Jang's own errata corrected the on-air variance math; credit assignment scales with sequence length.
  • Claude agents automated hyperparameter grinding and experiment execution; choosing the next experiment stayed human.
  • Porting search to LLMs needs a grounded value function and revisitable actions — both currently missing.

A 2017 loop, rebuilt to answer 2026 questions

Eric Jang left 1X Technologies, where he was VP of AI, in January 2026 for a sabbatical [2]; before that he spent six years at Google Brain and what is now Google DeepMind Robotics, where he coauthored QT-Opt, Google's large-scale off-policy system for robotic grasping [3]. The sabbatical's first public project is a rebuild of AlphaGo with modern tooling — an interactive tutorial published on 2026-04-28, an MIT-licensed repo called AutoGo, and a bot trained on donated compute [4][5] — and on 2026-05-15 he walked Dwarkesh Patel through the entire stack on a blackboard, from the rules of Go up to automated research [1].

The premise is that you sometimes understand the future better by stepping backward: AlphaGo remains the cleanest worked example of search, learning, and self-play operating as one loop, and the 2017 design solved a problem — credit assignment — that today's LLM reinforcement learning still routes around. The timing is deliberate, with reasoning models trained on outcome rewards dominating the industry, the automated-AI-researcher debate live, and Jang's own February 2026 essay arguing that thinking itself is becoming a computational primitive [6]. He opens with the hook that carries the whole project: thanks to LLM coding assistants, what once took a team of DeepMind scientists and millions of dollars can now be attempted for a few thousand dollars 2:00. The honest version of that claim is the more interesting one.

Two network heads make an impossible tree searchable

Go's difficulty is quantifiable. Under Tromp-Taylor rules — the unambiguous formulation all Go AIs train and score against [7]2:39 — a game runs roughly 250 to 300 moves with up to 361 choices each, and Jang sketches the naive game tree as something like 361 to the 300th power, far more paths than atoms in the universe, before immediately qualifying that symmetries and transpositions shrink it 9:5610:58. The exact count of legal positions, computed by John Tromp in January 2016, is about 2.08 × 10^170 [8]. Exhaustive search is out; the question is where to spend a finite budget of lookahead.

AlphaGo's answer is one small network wrapped in a search loop. The board enters as a three-plane image — black stones, white stones, empty points — and flows through a residual network Jang sizes at about ten layers and under 3 million parameters 37:5041:48. Two heads share that trunk: a policy head that outputs a distribution over the 361 points, and a value head that predicts win probability as a binary classification 31:5432:39. In his re-implementation, ResNets still beat transformers in this small-data regime because local convolutions match Go's spatial structure 32:39; the architecture's known weakness — connecting fights on opposite sides of the board — is what KataGo's global feature pooling was designed to patch, a finding Jang highlights from that paper 33:22[10].

Each move triggers a fresh Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) of 200 to 2,048 simulations, each the same four-step loop: select a child by the PUCT criterion — mean action value plus a prior-weighted exploration bonus that decays with visits — expand a new leaf, evaluate it with the value head instead of playing to the end, and back the result up the tree as a running mean 43:0545:1453:33. The division of labor is exact: the policy prior prunes the tree's breadth, the value net truncates its depth, and the final move is chosen not by value estimates but by how often search visited each child 27:1554:22.

Training is distillation of search, run after every game

The part Jang most wants the audience to see happens after the game. The raw policy makes a diffuse guess, MCTS refines it into a peakier visit distribution, and the training step simply teaches the policy head to predict that refined distribution while the value head learns to predict who actually won 1:00:241:02:461:10:50. DeepMind's own description of AlphaGo Zero was that it becomes "its own teacher" [11]; Jang's version is more mechanical — distillation amortizes about 1,000 simulations of search into the next forward pass, so the next search starts from a stronger prior and climbs higher on the same budget 1:03:34. He stresses that the implementation contains no explicit temporal-difference learning or dynamic programming: it is supervised learning on labels that search keeps improving, and the training is correspondingly stable 2:20:19.

In the vocabulary of Jang's robotics lineage, MCTS is a DAgger-style expert [13]: it revisits every state the policy reached and relabels it with a better action, so a lost 300-move game still yields 300 dense training targets rather than one terminal verdict 1:05:49.

1:06:26 "What MCTS is doing is saying: you played this game where you eventually lost, but on every single action, I'm going to give you a strictly better action that you should have taken instead."

Eric Jang

"Strictly" is the rhetorical engine of that framing, and the talk corrects it within two minutes: pressed by Patel, Jang concedes that "in practice, it is a heuristic" 1:07:59, and the AlphaGo Zero paper claims only that search "usually" selects stronger moves than the raw policy [12]. The failure mode he sketches is concrete: if self-play games always end in resignation, the replay buffer starves of resolved endgames, the value head degrades exactly where search grounds out, and the corrupted values back up through PUCT into a distribution worse than the prior 1:08:44. His fixes are engineering, not theory — force about 10% of games to full Tromp-Taylor resolution, and learn values on near-decidable endgames first so search can back them up into the murky midgame 1:09:301:12:07. The dependency is absolute: "Ultimately, MCTS will fall apart if you don't have a grounding function for the value" 1:14:41.

One version distinction matters, because the talk says "AlphaGo" loosely. The elegant loop — a single two-headed network, no rollouts, a policy trained on the search distribution — is AlphaGo Zero, from 2017. The 2016 system that beat Lee Sedol was initialized on human expert games 38:54, kept two separate networks 1:14:41, refined its policy with REINFORCE, and evaluated leaves by averaging the value net at equal weight with playouts by a separate fast rollout policy — details the later papers discarded [9][12]50:1652:04. Our reading: the elegance was the second iteration, reached only after a messier human-bootstrapped system worked — worth remembering when the loop is offered as a design lesson.

One label per move beats one bit through a straw

To show what the distillation buys, Jang runs the naive alternative: self-play where you simply imitate the winner, the closest board-game analog of modern LLM RL. Between two evenly matched policies, imagine 100 games of 300 moves each in which one side wins 51 1:26:34 and, in his deliberately stylized model, exactly one move was genuinely better play rather than luck 1:27:34 — winner-imitation then trains on tens of thousands of moves to propagate one move of real signal. Patel connects this to Andrej Karpathy's description of policy-gradient RL as "sucking supervision through a straw" 1:28:20, a line from Karpathy's October 2025 appearance on the same podcast [14]. Baselines and advantage estimation exist to cancel the neutral moves — Jang points to Schulman's Generalized Advantage Estimation as the canonical treatment 1:36:31[16] — but they estimate around the problem rather than removing it.

The information-theoretic version of the argument is the talk's sharpest: a learner should maximize bits per FLOP, which decomposes into samples per FLOP times bits per sample 2:12:59. Long-horizon agentic tasks crush the first factor, since a two-day trajectory buys a single verdict 2:12:34, while outcome rewards cap the second: a sampled pass/fail is worth at most a coin flip's entropy, and far less at the low pass rates where RL spends most of its training 2:14:442:16:46. A supervised label delivers −log p bits in one cross-entropy update, so an untrained model with a 100,000-token vocabulary would need on the order of 100,000 random tries to extract by RL what one label teaches directly 2:13:56. This is why Jang calls AlphaGo's objective beautiful: the policy imitates the full MCTS visit distribution — a soft target richer than any single action — and "you never have to initialize at a zero percent success rate" 2:19:232:20:19. Current LLM practice is the mirror image: the sequence is treated as one action 1:31:35, and GRPO-style outcome supervision broadcasts one normalized end-of-answer reward to every token as its advantage [15].

The honest footnote is that the adjacent on-air math failed. On air Jang derived that multi-step RL suffers variance growing quadratically with the horizon; four days after publication he posted a signed errata retracting it [17]. Under a single terminal reward, the single-step and multi-step REINFORCE estimators are identical; with per-token rewards, multi-step formulations actually have lower variance; the quadratic term attaches to sequence length, not to the formulation. Labs score whole sequences because per-token rewards for LLM tasks are misspecified — pass/fail is a property of the finished answer — not because multi-step RL is noisier [17]. Our reading: the correction deletes the wrong mechanism while strengthening the thesis, since the errata's bottom line is that credit assignment stays quadratically hard as generations grow long — and a public retraction within a week is the blackboard-to-podcast loop working as intended.

The transfer to LLMs fails at two named components

Jang's diagnosis of why this machinery does not port to language models is mechanical. PUCT's exploration bonus is computed from per-child visit counts, and with a vocabulary of roughly 100,000 tokens a reasoning tree almost never samples the same child twice, so the statistic the selection rule runs on is undefined in practice 1:48:36. The depth cut fails too: truncating search needs a cheap, grounded evaluator, and open-ended language tasks have no Tromp-Taylor scoring to bottom out on 1:47:11. He treats the Google tree-structured-reasoning work of 2023–2024 — most likely the Tree of Thoughts line [18] — as unresolved 1:46:18, and still expects forward search to return once those two pieces exist 1:47:11.

The same grounding requirement disciplines off-policy training. To saturate his GPUs, Jang sampled old board states from the dataset and re-ran MCTS relabeling on them offline — an architecture he maps onto the QT-Opt replay-buffer systems he helped build at Google 2:05:102:06:42[3]. It works only on states the current policy could plausibly reach; relabeling unreachable states wastes network capacity, which is his explanation for why the field converged on mostly on-policy setups that use off-policy data for advantage estimation at best 2:01:442:10:46.

Our reading: the bits-per-sample indictment has a circularity worth naming. The supervised alternative presupposes a teacher producing soft targets, and in AlphaGo Zero that teacher is manufactured by search itself — exactly the operator LLMs lack. As an account of why outcome RL is information-poor, the argument stands; as a prescription for LLM training, it restates the open problem: where does the better label come from?

The numbers hold up — a notch off, and self-corrected

This episode's claims are unusually checkable, and checking returns a consistent verdict: mechanisms sound, figures loose. KataGo, dated on air to 2020 with a 40x compute reduction 1:19, is a 2019 paper whose own headline claim is a 50x reduction over comparable AlphaZero-style pipelines, surpassing ELF OpenGo's final model in 19 days on fewer than 30 GPUs [10]. The compute-trend chart Patel cites, with AlphaGo Zero as an outlier at 3 × 10^23 FLOP 1:55:01, matches the era's canonical estimates, but Epoch AI's current database has since revised the AlphaGo-family figures down by orders of magnitude — the outlier framing survives while the number does not [20][21]. The scaling-laws papers casually dated to 2018 2:32:14 are from January 2020 (Kaplan et al.) and March 2022 (Chinchilla) [22][23]. On the other side of the ledger, Andy Jones's 2021 board-game study really did demonstrate the trade-off between train-time and test-time compute, three and a half years before reasoning models made it the industry's organizing idea [24]1:50:54.

The money comparison deserves its exact shape. Replicating AlphaGo Zero's 40-day run was priced at roughly $35 million in a widely cited 2018 cloud-TPU rental estimate [19]; Jang's project ran on about $10,000 donated by Prime Intellect — roughly $4,000 of exploration and $3,000 for the final run 1:55:47. But the strong bot was bootstrapped by best-response training against KataGo, and the from-scratch run was still being validated at recording 1:56:33. Jang flags the asymmetry himself:

1:55:47 "The compute required to be the first to do something is always much larger than the compute it takes to catch up. It's the same story playing out in LLMs."

Eric Jang

Our reading: the honest comparison is frontier exploration versus teacher-assisted catch-up — the asymmetry the DeepSeek moment dramatized for language models — and the same information theory explains it: every deployed strong model is a soft-target teacher for whoever comes second.

The agent grinds the metric; the human owns the experiment tree

The project doubles as a field test of automated research, because Jang ran much of it through Claude agents — mostly Opus 4.6 and 4.7, Anthropic's frontier models of February and April 2026 2:22:51[25][26]. What worked: open-ended hyperparameter optimization — noticing small gradients in a layer, rewriting the data loader, inventing augmentations — an almost grad-student-like ability to grind a performance metric 2:23:32, plus full experiment execution through a custom Claude skill named Experiment that takes an axes-level description of a question and returns plots and a report 2:24:12. What failed sits one level up: the agents rarely chose the next experiment well within a research track 2:24:48, missed the lateral move of abandoning a track entirely, and surfaced infrastructure bugs only when Jang asked the pointed question himself 2:25:28.

2:25:28 "They don't seem to be able to step back and do the lateral thinking of, 'Wait a minute, this track doesn't really make sense. Let's go back to first principles […]'"

Eric Jang

Three qualifiers keep this snapshot honest. Jang hedges on air that Mythos-class models may dissolve these gaps 2:25:28; Anthropic shipped Claude Sonnet 5 on 2026-06-30 and Opus 5 on 2026-07-24, both after recording, so the boundary is a May 2026 snapshot, not a law [27]; and the observations are one researcher's uncontrolled self-report, from a speaker building a public body of work around autoresearch whose compute donor sells GPU capacity and RL environments [28] — the donation is disclosed on air, and the right response is weighting, not dismissal.

For working researchers, the talk compresses into a playbook. Initialize near success — "In deep learning, initialization is everything" 38:54 — by finding the strongest available teacher before attempting anything from scratch. Verify the stack layer by layer, rules engine before supervised baseline before search, because search on garbage value predictions wastes compute 42:361:11:26. Do not run science on a broken artifact: Jang fit plausible-looking scaling-law curves while his MCTS labeling was buggy, and calls the exercise studying scaling laws on bad data 1:54:25. And distrust efficiency tricks: on modern GPUs he found KataGo's auxiliary objectives unnecessary given a strong initialization, replaced distributed replay-buffer infrastructure with a plain synchronous loop, and suspects compute multipliers are correlated and transitory as hardware improves 1:59:091:59:452:31:38 — flagged as a preliminary vibe guess, not a peer-reviewed claim 1:58:24.

Open questions, each with a resolution condition

The episode's forecasts come with built-in tests, which makes them trackable. Whether forward search returns to language models depends on the two missing components — a grounded, affordable value function and an exploration rule that does not require revisiting children; watch for a lab result that revives tree search over reasoning with a learned, grounded evaluator rather than an LLM judge 1:47:11. Whether the Bitter Lesson [29] — Sutton's argument that general methods riding compute beat handcrafted knowledge — has fully absorbed KataGo's tricks resolves on a public scoreboard: if the tabula rasa run matches the KataGo-bootstrapped bot for a few thousand dollars, the transitory-multiplier thesis gains real evidence; if it does not, the cost story shrinks to distillation being cheap. The AutoGo repo is where that answer will land [5]1:56:33. Whether research taste falls to scale is already testable by handing a newer frontier agent the open environment and watching for unaided experiment selection 2:25:28.

The deepest open question is the one the episode ends on. Go works as a gym for automated research because its outer loop is trivially verifiable and hard to reward-hack 2:26:082:27:28; for research that matters economically, Patel's counterpoint stands: we improve what we can measure, and economically useful discovery is not yet measured 2:33:00. Jang offers a deliberately non-rigorous bridge, that DeepMind's games experience positively transferred to its LLM era and game-trained automated researchers should transfer likewise 2:33:43; Patel counters that the same heritage was long blamed for Google's slow LLM start, and that the real payoff may have been the TPU tech tree 2:34:312:35:20. The conversation closes conceding that even the humans who lived a research strategy struggle to evaluate it in hindsight 2:35:20. Our reading: that concession, more than any variance formula, is the episode's real message about intelligence explosions — execution is automating quickly, while verifying direction, the outer loop of research itself, remains unsolved.
